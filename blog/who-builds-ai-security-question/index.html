Why Who Builds AI Is a Security Question

By Jax Scott, former Special Operations cyber warrant officer and founder of TheZaraAI

The short version

  • Attackers live in your blind spots. A team that thinks alike misses the same abuse cases.
  • NIST agrees. The AI Risk Management Framework asks for AI risk decisions to be informed by a diverse team.
  • The room is still narrow. In ISC2's latest study, 14% of participants said their security teams have no women at all.
  • Fix it on purpose: change who sits in threat modeling and red teaming, train people who were told AI is not for them, and nominate women for the work they already do.

When everyone designing a system shares the same blind spots, you do not get better AI. You get brittle AI. People often hear that as a diversity point. It is a security point first.

Security is a game of imagination

Every serious control starts with someone asking, "How could this be abused?" Threat modeling, red teaming and prompt injection testing all depend on the people in the room picturing what an attacker, a stalker or a fraudster would do with the system.

You can only test for the misuse you can imagine. If every person on the team has the same background, the same commute, the same risks in daily life, they will imagine the same attacks. The attacker does not share their blind spots. That gap is where incidents come from.

My take: AI makes this worse, not better. A model ships with behaviors nobody fully specified, and agents act on them at machine speed. The cases nobody thought to test are the ones that end up in the news.

This is in the framework, not just my opinion

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary guidance from the US standards body. Its Core says, under GOVERN 3.1, that decisions about mapping, measuring and managing AI risk should be informed by a diverse team, naming diversity of demographics, disciplines, experience, expertise and backgrounds. MAP 1.2 asks that the people establishing an AI system's context reflect demographic diversity and broad domain and user experience expertise, and that their participation is documented.

That is a risk control, written into the framework. Treat it like one.

The room is still narrow

ISC2's March 2026 look at women in its Cybersecurity Workforce Study counted 2,603 women among 16,029 participants. Asked to estimate the share of women on their own security teams, 14% of participants said their teams have none, and 21% said 10% or fewer. ISC2 is clear that these are perceptions, not a census. They still describe a lot of rooms where AI risk decisions get made with no women in them.

What leaders can do this quarter

  • Change who is in the threat model. Invite people from fraud, HR, customer support and legal into AI threat modeling and red team reviews. Document who took part, as MAP 1.2 asks.
  • Train the people closest to the risk. Teach AI to the people who were told it is not for them. Finance and HR staff are the ones a deepfake calls first.
  • Let women build, not just use. Give women on your security team time and budget to build agents and break them. That is where the next AI security leaders come from.
  • Sponsor, then nominate. Put names forward for stages, promotions and awards. I keep a list of awards and scholarships for women in AI and cybersecurity.

The short version

  • This is not hypothetical. An Arup employee paid out about $25 million after a video call where the "CFO" and colleagues were deepfakes.
  • The FBI counted more than 22,000 AI-related complaints in 2025, with adjusted losses over $893 million, in its 2025 Internet Crime Report.
  • Detection by eye is not a control. The FBI says AI-generated content is often difficult to identify. Train the process, not the eyeballs.
  • The fix is boring: call back on a number you already have, require two people for money, and make it safe to say no to the boss.

What is actually happening

A whole video call, faked

In 2024, engineering firm Arup confirmed that an employee in its Hong Kong office joined a video call in which the people who appeared to be the chief financial officer and several colleagues were AI-generated fakes. The employee made 15 transfers totaling about $25.6 million, Fortune reported.

A CEO impersonation that failed

Fraudsters set up a WhatsApp account with WPP CEO Mark Read's public photo and used it to arrange a Microsoft Teams meeting. In the meeting they used a voice clone and YouTube footage of another senior executive, and impersonated Read through the chat window, The Guardian reported. They asked an agency leader to set up a new business. It failed, and WPP credited the vigilance of its people.

What the FBI and FinCEN say

The FBI warned in December 2024 that criminals generate video for real-time chats with supposed company executives and use cloned voices to request money. In May 2025 it warned of text and AI-generated voice messages impersonating senior US officials, often paired with a request to move to another messaging platform.

The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report recorded 22,364 complaints with AI-related information and adjusted losses above $893 million. Business email compromise alone drew 24,768 complaints and about $3.05 billion in reported losses; the FBI notes that not all BEC is AI-enabled, but businesses reported more than $30 million lost to BEC involving AI.

FinCEN's November 2024 alert says criminals have reportedly impersonated executives to instruct staff to send large payments. Its red flags for banks verifying customers include a third-party webcam plugin during a live check, sudden "technical glitches" that push a switch to another channel, and refusal to use multifactor authentication.

Six scenarios to train for

  1. The urgent video call. A senior leader, and maybe others you know, on camera asking for a confidential transfer today.
  2. The voice note. A short message in the CEO's voice: "Call me on this number, it's urgent."
  3. The channel switch. "Let's move this to WhatsApp" or "my camera is broken, use the chat." The FBI warns about the move to another platform, and a version of this shows up in FinCEN's red flags.
  4. The secret deal. An acquisition or payment no one else can know about. Secrecy is the tell, because it stops you from checking.
  5. The bank detail change. A known vendor or executive asks to pay a new account. This is classic BEC with a better voice.
  6. The fake candidate. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report describes interviews where lip movement and audio do not line up, with the goal of getting onto the network.

Controls that beat a perfect fake

  • Call back on a number you already have. Never on the number, link or meeting in the request. The FBI gives the same advice.
  • Two people for money and access. No single person can move funds or change bank details on a call or chat alone.
  • Agree on a verification phrase. The FBI recommends a secret word for families; executive teams can do the same for high-risk requests.
  • Phishing-resistant MFA. FinCEN names it as a best practice for identity checks. Never read a code to anyone.
  • No exceptions for rank. Put it in writing: anyone can pause a request from the CEO to verify it, and will be thanked for it.

My take: Most deepfake training teaches people to spot a glitchy face. That is a losing game. The winning move is a process that does not care how real the voice sounds. If your CFO cannot be talked into a transfer by the real CEO on a call without a callback, a fake one will not get far either.

How to run the drill

Pick the three teams that move money or grant access: finance, HR and the executive assistants. Walk them through each scenario above, then run one live, announced exercise per quarter.
